**Why the drafts grew.** Repeated review rounds added machinery for edge cases the law does not require: legal-hold fences, erasure journals and confirmation tokens, restore-replay manifests, evidence-expiry clocks for evidence records, compatibility bridges spanning several minors, lookup-hash rehash programs, store-scanning verification suites and closure-evidence registers. The minimal program keeps every legally required capability and drops that machinery.

**What changed in substance.**
- Request tooling (export, erasure) moves to the Enterprise `privacy` module, following the maintainer's comment on #8593. The handlers stay in their owning core modules; the Enterprise runner calls them, so every core module an Enterprise client enables is covered.
- **Compliance target (owner decision, 2026-11-20):** an Enterprise deployment, meaning the Enterprise runner together with every core module the client enables, must provide every legally required function. The flows run in Enterprise; each core module ships its own handler. An OSS-only deployment is not a compliance target. Verified against the legal baseline: each required capability maps to a work package available in Enterprise builds.
- Restore-after-erasure, restriction, Art. 19 notification, RoPA, DPIA, tenant-exit return and inactive-subject review become documented procedures, which the law accepts.
- Encryption coverage, KMS fail-closed, search token redesign, queue sealing, cache encryption, alerting and auth hardening leave the GDPR program. They are Art. 32 hardening or conveniences and should be filed as ordinary issues.

## Legal baseline

Legend: **R** legally required capability · **P** a documented procedure is sufficient · **B** beyond the law.

**Role note.** The GDPR binds controllers (Art. 4(7)) and processors (Art. 4(8)), not software producers. Recital 78 only *encourages* producers. EDPB Guidelines 4/2019 §94 says producers are "not directly addressed in Article 25". The OSS vendor becomes a processor or controller only if it actually processes deployment data, for example through hosting, support access or **telemetry containing personal data**. So: no default phone-home with personal data. The market lever is Art. 28(1): controllers "shall use only processors providing sufficient guarantees". EDPB 4/2019 §96 also says controllers "should not choose producers or processors who do not offer systems enabling" compliance.

### Commonly mistaken as GDPR requirements

1. **"We must ship a self-service privacy portal (download my data / delete my account)."** No. Recital 63 says "where possible". EDPB 01/2022 §136 says manual routines are acceptable even at scale. CNIL says in-app rights handling "is not mandatory". What *is* required is that staff can actually fulfil requests within one month.
2. **"Data must be auto-deleted after a fixed period."** No fixed periods exist in the GDPR. The controller sets limits "for erasure or for a periodic review" (Recital 39). Automation is EDPB-recommended good design, not a legal precondition. The software must make deletion possible and must not hard-code retention.
3. **"Erasure means purging every backup immediately."** No. Put the data beyond use and re-apply the erasure on restore (EDPB CEF 2025 Issue 6; ICO, UK). Be transparent about it in the access response (EDPB 01/2022 §110).
4. **"Hashing, pseudonymising or masking a record counts as erasure or anonymisation."** No (Recital 26; WP216; CEF 2025 Issue 7). *SRB* (C-413/23 P) only relativises the position of a *recipient* who has no re-identification means. It does not help the controller who holds the key.
5. **"Every email needs an unsubscribe link and a List-Unsubscribe header."** Only *direct-marketing* emails need an opt-out (ePrivacy Art. 13(2),(4); GDPR Art. 21). Transactional and system emails don't. The `List-Unsubscribe` / one-click header is a Gmail/Yahoo bulk-sender rule (RFC 8058), not EU law.
6. **"Every site needs a cookie banner."** No, not if only strictly-necessary first-party storage is used: session, auth, CSRF, load-balancing, cart, language (ePrivacy Art. 5(3) second sentence; WP194; CNIL). The banner is triggered by optional analytics, tracking pixels or third-party embeds.
7. **"GDPR mandates encryption at rest / field-level encryption / MFA."** No. Art. 32 is risk-based and names encryption "as appropriate". MFA is not named, though ENISA and CNIL recommend it, and for SaaS admin access it is close to state of the art.
8. **"Personal data must stay in the EU / US providers are banned."** No. Chapter V allows transfers via adequacy (EU-US DPF, upheld in T-553/23, appeal pending) or SCCs plus assessment. The software only needs to let the controller choose or disable providers, and to document the data flows.
9. **"Data portability covers all data and needs a live API."** No. It is limited to data *provided by* the subject under consent or contract, excluding inferred/derived data. Direct transmission happens only "where technically feasible" (Art. 20(2)), and no format is prescribed (WP242).
10. **"Restriction needs a dedicated restricted flag on every table."** No. Recital 67 accepts moving the data elsewhere or making it unavailable to users. A visible marker plus edit and processing blocks, or a documented quarantine procedure, suffices.
11. **"The software must generate the RoPA / DPIA."** No. Both are the controller's (and processor's) documents (Art. 30, 35). A vendor data-flow description is helpful (B), and WP248 notes a producer DPIA can *inform* the deployer's own.
12. **"The OSS vendor is a processor for every deployment and must sign DPAs."** No. It is a processor or controller only if it actually processes deployment data (hosting, support access, telemetry with personal data). Art. 25 does not directly bind producers (EDPB 4/2019 §94).
13. **"Every breach must be reported within 72h."** No. Notification to the authority is required unless the breach is "unlikely to result in a risk" (Art. 33(1)), but *all* breaches must be documented internally (Art. 33(5)). A processor notifies the controller, not the authority.
14. **"Logs and audit trails are exempt because they serve security."** No. They are personal data (EDPB 9/2022 fn. 26) and need a defined, bounded retention (EDPB 4/2019 "Backups/logs"; CNIL: 6–12 months).
